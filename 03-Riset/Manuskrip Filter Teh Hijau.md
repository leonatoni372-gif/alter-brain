## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

Access to safe drinking water is Sustainable Development Goal 6, yet an estimated 2 billion people use contaminated sources (WHO/UNICEF, 2023). Industrial effluents discharge Pb, Cd, Cr(VI), Ni, and Zn into rivers and wells; chronic exposure causes neurological damage (Pb), renal failure (Cd), and carcinogenesis (Cr(VI)) (WHO, 2022, *Guidelines for Drinking-Water Quality*, 4th ed.).

Conventional treatments — reverse osmosis, membrane filtration, ion exchange — achieve high removal but demand capital, energy, and maintenance beyond the reach of rural households (Etim et al., 2024). Biosorption using agricultural waste has therefore attracted attention as a low-cost alternative (Soliman & Moustafa, review, *J. Mater. Res. Technol.*).

Tea (*Camellia sinensis*) is the world's most consumed beverage, generating massive leaf waste. Its surface chemistry — polyphenols (catechins), carboxyl, amine, and hydroxyl groups — binds metal ions via ion exchange and complexation (Ahluwalia & Goyal, 2005). Reported performances: Pb 96%, Fe 91%, Zn 72%, Ni 58% removal from 20 mg/L solutions with column capacity 73 mg Pb/g biomass (Ahluwalia & Goyal, 2005); Cu²⁺ removal >97% (Etim et al., 2022); Cr(VI) removal by used green tea leaves (Sage, *Air Soil Water Res.*); Ni biosorption optimum pH 5, dosage 3 g/50 mL (Etim et al., 2024, *Discover Chemistry*); Cd removal up to 82% with treated tea waste (Heliyon, 2024); and most recently, ordinary tea brewing shown to adsorb Pb/Cd meaningfully, time-dependently (Shindel et al., 2025, *ACS Food Sci. Technol.*).

**Gap:** Most studies use *waste* tea; few test *concentrated green tea extract* immobilized in a reusable filter matrix, and few combine general purification (turbidity/pathogen indicators) with heavy metal removal in one prototype. **This study:** (1) prepares green tea leaf concentrate; (2) builds a layered biofilter prototype; (3) quantifies purification + metal removal; (4) models isotherms/kinetics.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Materials
Fresh green tea leaves (local market, Indramayu); Pb(NO₃)₂, CdCl₂, K₂Cr₂O₇, FeCl₃, ZnSO₄ (analytical grade, for synthetic contaminated water); fine sand, gravel, cotton, activated carbon (filter layers); PVC column (d = 5 cm, L = 50 cm). Instruments: pH meter, TDS meter, turbidimeter; AAS (university laboratory) for metal analysis.

### 2.2 Preparation of green tea leaf concentrate
1. Wash 500 g fresh leaves, air-dry 3 days, oven-dry 60 °C to constant mass.
2. Grind, sieve (60 mesh).
3. Boil 100 g powder in 1 L distilled water (80 °C, 30 min), filter; evaporate to 200 mL concentrate.
4. Immobilize: mix concentrate with [DATA — e.g., "sodium alginate beads / dried powder packed between cotton layers"], dry 50 °C.

### 2.3 Filter prototype
Bottom→top: gravel (5 cm) → coarse sand (5 cm) → activated carbon (3 cm) → tea-concentrate layer (5 cm, variable under test) → fine sand (5 cm) → cotton. Flow: gravity-fed, flow rate [DATA] mL/min.

### 2.4 Experimental design
- **Batch test:** 50 mL metal solution (5–100 mg/L), adsorbent 1–4 g, pH 1–7 (HCl/NaOH), contact 10–120 min, 25–55 °C, 150 rpm.
- **Column test:** 1 L synthetic + 1 L well/river water samples; measure before/after: pH, TDS, turbidity, Pb, Cd, Cr, Fe, Zn.
- **Control:** identical column without tea layer.
- Removal efficiency: `%R = (C₀ − Ce)/C₀ × 100%`; capacity `qe = (C₀ − Ce)V/m`.
- Models: Langmuir `Ce/qe = 1/(qm·KL) + Ce/qm`; Freundlich `log qe = log KF + (1/n) log Ce`; pseudo-second-order `t/qt = 1/(k₂·qe²) + t/qe`.
